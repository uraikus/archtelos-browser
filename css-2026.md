# CSS: what the style engine implements, against the 2026 snapshot

The target is the **[CSS Snapshot 2026](https://www.w3.org/TR/css-2026/)**
(CLAUDE.md, §2). The snapshot sorts specifications into four classes;
this file takes them in its order and states, for each, what the engine
does. The work it implies is in [todo.md](todo.md).

**The engine column is read from the code**, not from the other
documents: all of `src/css/`, plus the places `src/layout/layout.f` and
`src/paint/paint.f` consume a computed value. A property the cascade
computes but neither reads counts as absent, because a page renders the
same either way. Nothing is excluded by it now: `overflow` was, until
the painter began clipping by it.

`www.w3.org` is refused by this network's egress policy — the proxy's
CONNECT is answered 403 — so the snapshot has to be supplied to a
session rather than fetched, as it was for this one.

## The headline

The official definition of CSS is 24 specifications. **The engine
implements no part of 1 of them** and part of the other 23, two of those
completely — Namespaces 3 and Conditional 3. The one it does not touch
is the one it cannot: **Easing 1** describes the timing functions of
transitions and animations, which need a clock and a repaint loop rather
than a property.

**Compositing and Blending 1** is the specification that looks like it
belongs beside Easing 1 and does not. Its blending half is blocked --
the runtime sets `CAIRO_OPERATOR_SOURCE` at every draw with no call to
change it, and a blend mode would need the destination pixel besides --
but `isolation` is in it too, and `isolation: isolate` creates a
stacking context, which needs no blending and changes a pixel here.

Of the three lower classes, Grid is substantially implemented, Selectors
4's functional pseudo-classes are, Fragmentation 3 has the break
controls a column context can act on, Masking 1 has `clip-path`, Shapes 1 has `shape-outside`, and Transforms 2 and Containment 2
have the parts that are not three-dimensional or dependent on knowing
what is on screen. `@layer` orders the cascade, and Color 5 is
both of its features.

Three runners in `tests/conformance/` put numbers on it, and each is a
floor the suite will not let drop:

| Measure | Now |
|---|---|
| CSS properties that change what renders, of the 418 the instrument can grade | **284** |
| HTML elements whose default `display` matches Chromium's, of 122 | **122** |
| CSS selectors matching the same elements as Chromium, of 129 | **129** |

The property count uses the same rule as this file: a property the
cascade computes but neither layout nor paint reads is not implemented,
because the page renders the same either way. Nothing falls to it, so
the figure above is the instrument's own.

## 1. The official definition

Everything here is stable, and the snapshot's own framing is that this
is CSS "in its most complete form". The gaps in this table are the ones
that matter first.

| Specification | Engine |
|---|---|
| **CSS Level 2** | The bulk of what works: **CSS2 §9.9's painting order** -- within a stacking context a box's own background and border come first, then its negative-`z-index` descendants, then its in-flow content, then the positioned descendants at zero and above; a negative descendant of a box that is not a stacking context is painted by the nearest ancestor that is, which is what puts it behind that box's background. A page that declares no negative `z-index` takes the path it always took. Steps 3, 4 and 5 are three walks of the subtree rather than one walk in document order: the in-flow block-level descendants' own decoration, then the non-positioned floats, then every box's lines -- so a float paints over an in-flow block written after it and a line of text paints over both, which one walk cannot produce, because an anonymous block holding nothing but inline content is a block-level descendant while what is in it is step 5. A box that paints as one unit is handed over whole and the walk does not descend into it: a positioned box, a nested stacking context, a replaced element, and anything that paints through a layer -- `overflow`, paint containment, a `clip-path` -- since a layer is built and blitted once rather than three times. The two extra walks of the box tree cost nothing measurable, because the first walk writes on each box which step paints it and the other two read that integer rather than asking again -- asking again cost 7 ms of a 20 ms paint, and collecting the boxes into a list instead cost 523 (benchmarks.md, FINDINGS.md finding 41). No honest flag can skip the inline walk: the second fixture that measures it has no float on it at all. The outlines follow, in a pass of their own above all the in-flow content and below anything positioned -- which is where Chromium draws them, measured against all four things an outline can overlap (todo.md), rather than at the very end §9.9's wording suggests; a document that declares no outline does not walk for them. The pass is over the in-flow content rather than over everything: a float's own outline travels with the float at step 4, so inline content pulled over the float covers its outline too, which is what Chromium does. A clipping box's own outline goes on after its layer is blitted, an outline lying outside the border box that the layer would otherwise cut away. **Hit testing runs those passes backwards**, one function shared with the painter with every "paint" replaced by "answer if it is there", so a click and a pixel cannot drift apart -- todo.md carries the five overlaps that measure it. Then: normal flow with margin collapsing, `overflow` in all of `visible`, `hidden`, `clip`, `scroll` and `auto` -- the last two reserving fifteen pixels inside the padding box for a scrollbar and painting one there, `scroll` always and `auto` only where the content overflows, which costs those boxes a second layout of their content. Such a box scrolls on both axes: the wheel over it moves its content down and a wheel tilted sideways moves it across where the window system reports one, either thumb can be taken hold of and dragged, and both follow the content -- the painter draws it from the same four functions the pointer is tested against, so where it looks and where it can be grabbed are one rectangle rather than two formulas that agree -- and hit testing adds the offset back so a link inside it is clickable where it looks. A horizontal `auto` bar is raised by a line of text too long to break as well as by a child box reaching past the edge, which is what Chromium does: a 100x60 box of one unbreakable word has a client height of 45 in both. The offset is kept by element id, because a box tree lasts one layout and a scroll position outlives several -- percentage heights resolved against a definite containing block and `auto` where there is none (§10.5), with `min-height` and `max-height` taking them the same way, inline formatting and line boxes -- an inline box has its own margin, border and padding on all four sides (§8.4), drawn on the content area rather than on the line box, so a taller or shorter line height moves neither; the opening side goes on the fragment that begins a broken inline and the closing side on the one that ends it, and a fragment between them carries neither, which is what `box-decoration-break: slice` means, while `clone` gives every fragment both; none of the four changes the line height, so an inline's border paints outside its line and leaves the block exactly as tall. The opening side is the physical left one, which is right in left-to-right text and mirrored in right-to-left, for the same reason the bidirectional reordering stops at a text fragment -- table layout (§17) in both algorithms — automatic, and the fixed one that takes its column widths from the first row and never measures a cell's content — with `caption-side` and `empty-cells`, list markers inside or outside the box, restyled by `::marker` -- a colour on it moves nothing, a `font-size` on it makes a marker as wide as one the item's own font size would have made, and a `content` replaces the label with its own string, which is where `content: counter(list-item)` belongs -- and **positioning** (§9.3) — `relative` offsets a box and its descendants without disturbing the flow, `absolute` and `fixed` leave the flow and resolve against the nearest positioned ancestor or the viewport, all four inset properties work, and an inset left `auto` resolves to the box's **static position** (§10.3.7) — where it would have been in flow, which the flow records as it walks past, so an inline-level box lands where the inline would have been and a block-level one among inline content on the line after it, and `z-index` orders positioned siblings. **Floats** (§9.5) work too: a float is placed at the edge of its containing block after any float already there, the line boxes beside it are shortened so text wraps around it, a block box's own position and width ignore it, and `clear` moves a box below the floats on a side. A float with `width: auto` is shrink-to-fit — `min(max(min-content, available), max-content)` (§10.3.5) — so it hugs its content, and a single word wider than the space available makes it wider than its container rather than breaking the word. **Generated content** (§12) works: `::before` and `::after` generate boxes from `content`, which takes strings, `attr()`, `counter()`, `counters()` and the four quote keywords, and `counter-reset` and `counter-increment` maintain counters with the standard's scoping — an instance is in force for the element that reset it and that element's descendants, and for its following siblings as well unless an instance of the same name was already in scope, in which case it is the element's own subtree and no further. `quotes` gives the strings for `open-quote` and `close-quote`, at a depth that runs over the whole document in document order rather than following element nesting, repeating the last pair past the end of the list; `<q>` gets a pair from the user-agent stylesheet. `url()` in `content` generates a replaced box at the image's intrinsic size, in the order it was written among the strings beside it, and a url that does not load generates no box at all rather than a placeholder; a `width` on such a pseudo-element does not resize the image, and its margin, border and padding surround the whole run. On an **ordinary element** a `content` naming an image replaces the element's contents with it: the element becomes a replaced element sized by the ordinary rules from the image's natural size, keeping its own background, border and declared width and height, and its children are not rendered. A `content` holding anything but a `url()` replaces nothing there, which is what Chromium does with a string. A counter is always rendered in decimal. **`overflow: hidden`** clips a box's descendants to its padding box, painted through an offscreen image because the canvas has no clip region. An image has no path API (FINDINGS.md), so a `border-radius` inside such a subtree is filled a row at a time from the same span function the shadows ask for rather than by a bezier: the corners come out where they belong, hard-edged instead of antialiased, within a pixel of Chromium's. `::first-letter` (§5.12.2) styles the first letter of a block's first line, taking preceding punctuation with it and skipping leading whitespace. `::first-line` (§5.12.1) styles the characters that end up on the first line, metrics included: the cascade computes, for the block and every inline inside it, the style it would have under the fictional element the standard describes, and the line's fragments and its strut are given those styles as the line is filled, so a first line set larger is taller and holds fewer words. Where a block's inline content sits in anonymous boxes -- beside block-level siblings -- the rule belongs to the first of them and to no other, which is what Chromium does. The restricted property set the standard defines for the pseudo-element is not enforced. `clip` (§11.1.2) cuts an absolutely positioned box to a rectangle, through the same machinery as `clip-path`. `orphans` and `widows` (§13.3.1) constrain where a paragraph may be broken; there are no pages here, so they act where the only fragmented context is, between the columns of a multi-column container. **Paged media** (§13) works: `@page` declares the page box, with `size` in the ten named sheets -- A5 to A3, B5, B4, the two JIS sizes, letter, legal and ledger -- or one or two lengths, turned by `portrait` and `landscape`, and `margin` in the one-to-four-value form with percentages of the page box. `@page :first`, `:left` and `:right` declare it for particular pages, the first page being a right-hand one, and a named `@page` for the pages a `page` property sends there; the selectors are weighed as the triple Paged Media 3 §3.5 gives, so `:first` outranks `:right` however they are ordered, and rules accumulate rather than replace one another. `size: auto` is US Letter, which is the sheet Chromium prints on beside it. A page is a fragmentation container like a column, so pagination is the column algorithm over a different one: the same units, the same rules about where a break is allowed, and the same search for the nearest allowed point -- with the height asked for one page at a time, because `@page :first` can make the first sheet a different size from the rest. CSS2's `page-break-before`, `page-break-after` and `page-break-inside` are the three fragmentation properties under their older names (Fragmentation 3 §4.4), so they are renamed where a declaration is applied rather than implemented again, which makes the cascade between an old spelling and a new one one property's cascade. A change of `page` between two siblings forces a break into the named run and out of it again. A box taller than the sheet is cut at the page's edge rather than left to run off it. `--print` writes one image per page, and `@media print` is the medium such a render answers to, so a document's print stylesheet is the one that applies to its pages. The **margin boxes** (Paged Media 3 §5) are all sixteen: each is parsed out of the `@page` body, resolved per page by the same selector specificity the page box uses -- so a `@page :first` rule's `@top-center` replaces the general one on the first sheet and leaves it in force on the rest -- and painted in its band from its own `content`, which takes strings, `counter(page)` and `counter(pages)`, concatenated in the order written. Each box takes the default `text-align` and `vertical-align` §5.2 gives it, so a corner aligns *inward*, toward the page content, an edge box is left, centred or right within the region between the corners, and the three down each side are top-, middle- and bottom-aligned there; a `text-align` or `vertical-align` on the box itself overrides them. A margin box inherits from the **root element** rather than from `body`, which is what the page context means, and reads a `color` and a `font-size` of its own on top of that; `content: none` and an empty box draw nothing. `break-before: left`, `right`, `recto` and `verso` are honoured as sides rather than as plain page breaks: each ends a page, and where the next page would fall on the wrong side a blank one is generated to carry it over, which is the "one or two page breaks" of §13.3.1. The first page is a right page, so odd pages are right and even ones left -- the same parity `@page :left` and `:right` are selected by, kept in one function so the two cannot disagree. A page generated that way is the only page here with nothing on it, and `@page :blank` selects those and no others. **Chromium generates no such page**: it prints all four keywords as a plain `page` break, which is measured in todo.md and is a disagreement entered deliberately. One part is missing: a named page cannot take a different `size` from the first, because the document is laid out once at the first page's width and pages are strips of that one layout. A margin that collapses all the way through to the root is dropped rather than moving the document down |
| **CSS Syntax 3** | Partial, and not by this algorithm. There is no tokenizer in the standard's sense: parsing is index scanning. Comment stripping runs first, and it knows the three places §4.3 says a `/*` cannot begin a comment: inside `'...'`, inside `"..."` and inside an unquoted `url(...)`, with a backslash escaping the next character in each. It knows the other direction too, which is the easier one to get wrong -- a comment ends at the **first** `*/` whatever is inside it, so a quote there is ordinary text and `/* "*/` really does leave a string open and swallow the rest of the sheet, as it does in Chromium. A stylesheet with no `/*` in it at all pays one search, as before. An escape in an identifier is decoded as §4.3.7 says: a backslash before a non-hex character stands for that character, and one before up to six hex digits stands for the code point they name, with a single following whitespace consumed as the escape's terminator rather than left as a combinator. Zero, a surrogate and anything past the last code point become U+FFFD. A code point above ASCII decodes to the character itself, because the document's own escapes are expanded by the tokenizer and its attributes hold real characters -- the two have to spell a name the same way for a selector to reach it. An identifier with no backslash in it is handed straight back. A CDO (`<!--`) and a CDC (`-->`) are ignored where a stylesheet's own rules are read, which is §5.4.1's `top-level` flag: an at-rule's body and a nested rule's body are parsed with it unset. They are tested only where a rule or a declaration begins, because a `-->` that follows name characters is not a CDC -- the identifier takes both hyphens and the `>` left over is a child combinator, which is what Chromium's `selectorText` for `a-->b` says |
| **CSS Style Attributes** | Yes. The `style` attribute is parsed per element, though it is treated as an origin above author rules rather than as a specificity |
| **Media Queries 3** | Every feature the level defines, in the boolean form as well as with a value: `width`, `height`, `device-width`, `device-height` and their `min-` and `max-` forms; `orientation`; `aspect-ratio` and `device-aspect-ratio`, compared as two whole numbers rather than two divisions, so 800 by 600 is `4/3` exactly; `color`, `color-index` and `monochrome`, which say eight bits a component, no colour table and not monochrome; `resolution` in `dpi`, `dpcm`, `dppx` and `x`, which says 96 to the inch; and `grid` and `scan`, which describe devices this is not. What it calls the device is its own window: there are no screen metrics to ask for, and a page asking about the device is deciding whether it is on a phone, which the window size answers as well as the screen does. `hover` and `pointer` answer `hover` and `fine`, because this browser opens a window with a pointer in it — headless Chromium answers `none`, which is a fact about that process rather than about the standard. The remaining gap is time rather than features: a query is evaluated when the sheet is parsed, so resizing the window does not re-evaluate it |
| **CSS Conditional 3** | Both. `@supports` evaluates its condition: a declaration is supported when the property is one the cascade computes and the value uses no function the engine cannot resolve. `not`, `and`, `or` and nesting are handled; `selector()` reports unsupported. The list of properties it answers from is checked against the property instrument on every run: a property that changes what renders and is not on the list, or is on the list and changes nothing, fails the suite, because a hand-written list beside a measurement drifts from it in both directions and silently |
| **Selectors 3** | Every selector from this level that the instrument asks, it gets right: all 129 entries in `tests/conformance/css-selectors.txt` match exactly the elements Chromium matches, and 68 of them are from Selectors 4. Type, `*`, `#id`, `.class`, all six attribute operators -- whose name and value are both CSS Syntax 3 tokens, so `[data\-x=a\ b]` names the attribute `data-x` and the value `a b`, and a `]`, a `[` or the quote itself inside a quoted value is part of the value rather than the end of the selector -- all four combinators, the full `An+B` grammar in `:nth-child()`, `:nth-last-child()`, `:nth-of-type()` and `:nth-last-of-type()`, the `first`/`last`/`only` child and of-type pseudo-classes, `:root`, `:empty`, `:link`, `:enabled`, `:disabled`, `:checked`, `:lang()`, and `:not()` with one compound. A rule whose selector list contains anything unparseable is dropped whole, as §4 requires. `:target` parses and matches nothing, which is right for a document never navigated to a fragment. **`::before`, `::after`, `::first-letter` and `::first-line` all work**, in both the one- and two-colon spellings, and **`::marker`** and **`::placeholder`** work in the two-colon spelling the standard gives them -- `:marker` with one colon is not a pseudo-element, so it matches nothing. `::placeholder` styles the text an `<input>` shows for its `placeholder` attribute: the user agent's stylesheet declares the grey `#757575` **on the pseudo-element**, which is why the input's own `color` cannot reach it -- an inherited value loses to any declaration, whatever origin the declaration comes from -- while the font properties do inherit and a declaration on the pseudo-element wins over both. Measured in Chromium, including that `display` on it is ignored. It is the only pseudo-element here computed on demand rather than in the style pass, because it applies to one kind of element in one state and layout is what knows which those are. `::selection`, `::backdrop` and `::target-text` need a selection, a top layer and a search this browser does not have |
| **CSS Namespaces 3** | All of it. `@namespace` binds a prefix, or a default namespace when it names none, and a selector's namespace part is honoured: `ns|E` for a declared prefix, `*|E` for any namespace, `|E` for no namespace, and a bare `E` for any namespace until a default is declared and then only that one. A prefix nobody declared makes the selector invalid, so it matches nothing rather than being read as a type. Every element in an HTML document is in the XHTML namespace, so all of this comes down to one string comparison — which is also why the whole specification can be honoured rather than approximated |
| **CSS Cascade 4** | Substantially. Specificity is the triple (ids, classes and attributes and pseudo-classes, types), packed base 1024 so that an integer comparison is the lexicographic one. Importance inverts the origin order, so an important user-agent declaration outranks an important author one. `inherit` takes the parent's computed value, `initial` the initial value, and `unset` whichever of the two the property calls for. **`revert` rolls the property back to the value the previous origin gave it**: the matches arrive sorted, so the user-agent origin's declarations are the ones before the first weight at or above the origin boundary, and copying the map there is the whole of the bookkeeping -- done only on a page that says `revert` at all. A rollback that finds nothing in that origin is `unset`, which is what removing the declaration leaves. **`revert-layer`** rolls back one step rather than the whole origin: the matches carry the origin and the layer packed into one rank, so a change in that rank is a layer boundary, and the map as it stood before each layer began is what the keyword resolves against. An unlayered declaration is in the implicit outer layer, which comes after every named one, so reverting one of those lands on the last named layer -- green where `revert` beside it lands on black, which is Chromium's answer too. A style attribute ranks above an unlayered rule, so `revert-layer` there rolls back to that rule and `revert` past it to the user-agent sheet: 400 and 700 on the same `<b>`. Resolving after the declarations are applied rather than at each one is what makes a shorthand work, because `applyDecl` has expanded it into longhands by then -- **every shorthand this engine reads is expanded there**, so a shorthand and a longhand of the same property occupy one key and the cascade decides between them rather than whichever the reader asks for last -- and **a CSS-wide keyword on a shorthand sets every one of those longhands**, which the four-sides shorthands got free by passing their value through unparsed and the five that parse a value into parts had to be told. `font: revert` read the keyword as a font family and set the weight to `normal`; an audit of twenty shorthands, in todo.md, is what found the other four. `all` sets every property at once: it drops every declaration before it in the block, and for `initial` computes the element as though it had no parent, which is what every default in the cascade already means by "root"; `all: revert` puts the previous origin's declarations back, `all: revert-layer` the previous layer's, and `unset` needs nothing beyond the dropping. `direction` and `unicode-bidi` are left alone, as the standard says. A CSS-wide keyword is a valid value for every property, so `display` -- the one property validated where it is applied, because by then the declaration it beat is gone -- lets one through, and `display: inherit` takes the parent's. Missing: `all: inherit`, which would have to give a *non-inherited* property the parent's value and so needs the parent style copied field by field |
| **CSS Values and Units 3** | Partial. Units: unitless, `px`, `%`, `em`, `rem`, `pt`, `pc`, `in`, `cm`, `mm`, `q`, `ex`, `ch`, `cap`, `ic`, `vw`, `vh`, `vmin`, `vmax`, and from Values and Units 4 `lh` and `rlh`, the `vi` and `vb` axes, and the small, large and dynamic viewport units, which are three names for this viewport because nothing here slides away to tell them apart. `ex`, `ch` and `cap` are ratios of the font size measured for the face this engine renders in, because the runtime reports no x-height, no zero advance and no cap height: 0.547, 0.600 and 0.733, each read twice — `ch` against this engine's own measurement of a `0` and against Chromium's `10ch`, `ex` against rasterised ink and against Chromium's `10ex`, and `cap` against the two Chromium surfaces that measure it, which agree to a tenth of a pixel across the range. `cap` and `ex` take the same constants `text-box-edge` reads rather than carrying their own. `ic` is an em, which is what Chromium measures at every size. Angles are read wherever one is taken -- a gradient's direction, `rotate()` -- in `deg`, `grad`, `rad` and `turn`. `lh` is the element's own computed line height and `rlh` the root element's: the cascade computes `line-height` before every other length, so the unit reads it off a global that is already set -- and that still holds the *parent's* while `line-height` itself is being computed, which is what the unit means there, the way `em` inside `font-size` means the parent's font size. Under `line-height: normal` it is what a line box actually comes out at, 1.2 times the font size, which is 19px at 16px in this engine and in Chromium alike. Resolutions are read in a media query, in `dpi`, `dpcm` and `dppx`. `calc()` is implemented: a sum of products over lengths and plain numbers, with nested parentheses and nested `calc()`, and a result that carries a pixel part and a percentage part separately so `calc(100% - 2em)` waits for the containing block. The standard's invalid cases are rejected, among them a `-` without spaces around it and a length added to a number. `min()`, `max()` and `clamp()` come from Values and Units 4 and work wherever a length is read, `calc()` included. An argument list of nothing but pixels is folded at parse time, so `min(10px, 20px)` is an ordinary pixel length and works in the places that take one by kind rather than through `resolveLen` — the places a `calc()` is refused. A percentage cannot fold, because the comparison happens *after* the percentage is resolved: `min(50%, 100px)` is 100 against a 400px base and 50 against a 100px one, so the operands are kept and the answer is worked out where the base is, which reaches every property resolved through `resolveLen` and no others. Four rules are Chromium's rather than the grammar's: `clamp()`'s minimum wins over its maximum, a bare number is not a length there and zero is not exempt, `min()` and `max()` take a single argument while `clamp()` takes exactly three, and a trailing comma is invalid. Nesting works both ways; a deferred comparison inside a `calc()` is refused rather than approximated, because a `calc()`'s running value is a pixel part and a percentage part and a deferred comparison is neither until the base is known. `attr()` is implemented, which is the whole of what Values 3 allows it to do: the specification permits it in `content` and nowhere else, and the typed form that reaches other properties belongs to Values 5. A missing attribute generates the empty string rather than dropping the declaration, the attribute's name folds and its value does not, and it sits among the strings beside it in the order written — all of which `tests/unit/test_counters.f` now checks, where nothing checked it before. Missing: the time and frequency units, which belong to the animations this engine does not run. `rem` multiplies the root element's computed font size and `vh` the real viewport height |
| **CSS Custom Properties 1** | Yes. Custom properties are kept, inherit, and `var(--name, fallback)` substitutes them wherever a value is read, including inside another custom property. An unresolvable `var()` with no fallback makes the declaration invalid rather than empty, as the standard requires. A vendor prefix is still dropped |
| **CSS Box Model 3** | Yes, in substance: all four margins and paddings, and margin collapsing, which CSS2 §8.3.1 still owns |
| **CSS Color 4** | Substantially: `transparent`, `currentcolor`, all four hex forms, `rgb()`, `rgba()`, `hsl()`, `hsla()` with either separator and alpha, all 148 named colors, all nineteen system colors, `opacity`, and the wider color spaces — `hwb()`, `lab()`, `lch()`, `oklab()`, `oklch()` and `color()` over `srgb`, `srgb-linear`, `display-p3`, `a98-rgb`, `prophoto-rgb`, `rec2020`, `xyz`, `xyz-d65` and `xyz-d50` — each with `none` components, percentages on the standard's own scales, a hue in degrees, radians, gradians or turns, and a slash alpha. They resolve to a packed sRGB integer through the standard's conversion matrices, clamped per channel where the color falls outside the sRGB gamut rather than gamut-mapped, which is what Chromium paints for these colors too. No `<color>` interpolation, since nothing here animates. `opacity` is not inherited: its computed value is the element's own, and a separate accumulated value carries group opacity to the painter, which approximates it rather than compositing a layer. The color syntax is graded by tests/unit/test_color4.f, whose expected pixels were derived twice over — from the standard's §17 pseudocode in a separate script, and from the pixel headless Chromium paints — because the property instrument counts `color` as implemented whatever syntax reaches it |

| CSS Motion Path 1 | All five properties. `offset-path` takes `ray()`, `circle()`, `ellipse()`, `polygon()` and `path()`; `offset-distance` a length or a percentage of the path's own length; `offset-rotate` `auto`, `reverse` and an angle beside either; `offset-anchor` which point of the box sits on the path, `auto` being the transform origin rather than the centre; and `offset-position` where a ray begins. The whole effect is one translation, `laid-out top-left + P - offset-anchor`, with the turn taken about `P`, and the `transform` property applies inside it. The path's coordinates are the element's own, not the containing block's. A circle and an ellipse start at three o'clock and run clockwise; a polygon closes itself; `path()` reads `M`, `L`, `H`, `V` and `Z` in either case, and the curve commands as well — `C` and `S`, `Q` and `T`, and `A` — each flattened into the same polyline at sixty-four segments a curve, which holds a hundred-pixel curve to well under a pixel and leaves the arc-length lookup unchanged. A quadratic is sampled as the cubic whose controls are two thirds of the way from each end to it, so there is one sampler rather than two; `S` and `T` reflect the previous curve's control point about the current point, and the current point itself where the command before was not of that kind; and `A` is converted from its two endpoints to a centre and two angles (SVG 1.1 §F.6.5), with radii too small for the chord scaled up until they fit rather than treated as invalid. More than one subpath: a second `M` begins one, the path's length is the sum of them and a distance walks them in order with nothing joining them, so a distance landing exactly on a join is the end of the subpath before it. `Z` closes the subpath it is in rather than the path, and the pairs after an `M`'s first are a line (SVG §8.3.2). A distance wraps round a path that is a single closed subpath and clamps at the ends of anything else, which is measured rather than reasoned about. A ray's length under a percentage distance, and a percentage inside a shape, resolve against the parent box, where the standard says the containing block -- the same rectangle whenever the parent is it. The ray sizing keywords follow the standard and not Chromium, which answers them as though only the top and left sides of the containing block existed (todo.md has the numbers). `inset()` is not a path here, because Chromium puts a start point on it and then never moves along it. `url(#id)` travels the `d` of the SVG `<path>` it names -- the path is never drawn, and does not have to be, since the element is in the DOM with its attributes whether or not anything renders SVG. A reference that resolves to nothing is an **empty** path and not `none`, which is measured rather than read off the standard's wording: the offset still applies and centres the box on the origin, where `none` leaves it where the flow put it. A `url()` also takes a `<circle>`, an `<ellipse>` and a `<polygon>`, each of which is the shape the cascade already builds and the motion code already travels, so the SVG spelling lands exactly where the CSS function of the same geometry lands. `<rect>`, `<line>` and `<polyline>` are travelled too, and needed no new machinery: a rect is the polygon of its four corners clockwise from (x, y), and the other two are path data, which is open where a `polygon()` closes itself. A `<rect>` carrying `rx` or `ry` has its corners rounded, as SVG 1.1 §9.2's own equivalent path -- four sides joined by four quarter-ellipse arcs from (x + rx, y) -- which is path data and so needs nothing the `A` command did not already provide. Each radius defaults to the other and is clamped to half its own side; a zero in either axis is sharp, and a negative value is the other axis. ## What this changes about the plan

**The snapshot's classification and this project's priorities do not
agree, and the classification wins on two counts.** Custom properties
with `calc()`, flexbox, and CSS Images are in the official definition of
CSS, not in some forward-looking tier: an engine missing them is missing
stable CSS, not modern CSS. Conversely `:is()`, `:where()`, `:has()` and
`@layer` ordering sit in the two lowest classes, so they are further
down the list than their prominence suggests.

**Positioning is official-definition work too**, through CSS2 §9.3
rather than through Positioned Layout 3. So are floats, `overflow`
clipping and generated content. The four largest holes in the engine are
all CSS2 chapters.

**The cascade bugs are fixed.** Specificity is compared as a triple,
importance inverts the origin order, `inherit` takes the parent's value,
`@supports` evaluates its condition, `text-decoration` and `opacity` no
longer inherit, `rem` and `vh` resolve against the real root font size
and viewport, and an unparseable selector drops its whole rule. What
remains of Cascade 4 is `revert` and `all`.

**A corpus is still the missing instrument.** The HTML parser went from
20% to 93% because someone else's tests said what was wrong; `css/` in
web-platform-tests is the equivalent, and the open question is how much
of it runs without script.
